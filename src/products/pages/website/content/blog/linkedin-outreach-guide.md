**Short answer:** good LinkedIn outreach is a small, relevant list, a short honest first message, a calm follow-up and a pace you could keep up by hand. The tools only change how much typing you do. They do not change whether the other person wants to hear from you.

This guide walks through the whole loop: who to contact, how to look when they check your profile, what to send, how to follow up, how much volume is sensible and what to measure. It is for founders, sales people, recruiters and anyone who needs to start conversations on LinkedIn.

## 1. Start with who, not what

Write one sentence describing the person you want to reach: a job title, a kind of company and a reason they would care. "Heads of Talent at Series A fintechs hiring engineers" is a list. "Decision makers" is not.

A narrow list makes everything else easier. Your note can mention something real, your offer fits, and your acceptance rate stays healthy. Build the list from a LinkedIn search with filters, from companies you already follow, or from people who view your profile. In Spurly, the [lead finder](/product/lead-finder) covers all of those.

## 2. Make your profile do the second half of the work

Before accepting, many people open your profile. It should answer three questions in a few seconds: who are you, what do you do and why are you writing to me?

- A real photo and a headline that says what you do for whom, not only a job title.
- An About section in plain language with one line on how to work with you.
- Recent activity that shows you are a person: a post, a comment, a reply.

## 3. The connection request

Keep it short. LinkedIn caps the note at 300 characters, and the preview is shorter, so aim for two sentences. A good note has a hook, a reason and no pitch.

> Hi Priya, I saw your post on hiring engineers in 2026. I recruit for fintech teams and would like to follow your work. Happy to connect?

See [ten connection request examples](/blog/linkedin-connection-request-examples) for different situations, and [templates you can copy](/templates/linkedin-connection-request-messages). To write several in different tones, try the [free connection note generator](/tools/linkedin-connection-request-generator).

## 4. The first message after they accept

Many people send a pitch the moment someone accepts. Do the opposite: say thanks, say why you connected and ask one easy question. Save the offer for when they have shown interest.

Our [cold message structure guide](/blog/linkedin-cold-message-structure) breaks this into four parts you can reuse.

## 5. Follow up without being annoying

Most conversations start on the second or third touch, not the first. Plan a short sequence: a thank-you, a message with something useful, and one or two brief reminders spaced a week or more apart. Stop the moment someone replies. If they say no, thank them and stop. The details are in [the follow-up sequence guide](/blog/linkedin-follow-up-message-sequence).

## 6. Pace yourself

LinkedIn publishes no fixed limits, and its rules restrict automation. Whether you send by hand or with a tool, keep your volume modest, spread it over the week and avoid sudden jumps. Our guides on the [weekly invitation limit](/blog/linkedin-weekly-invitation-limit) and [daily limits](/blog/linkedin-automation-limits-per-day) give the figures vendors suggest and the ones Spurly enforces, and [is LinkedIn automation safe?](/blog/is-linkedin-automation-safe) covers the risk honestly.

## 7. Measure what matters

Track four numbers each week:

| Number | How to get it | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Requests sent | Count | Your volume |
| Acceptance rate | Accepted divided by sent | Whether the list and note fit; see [acceptance rate](/blog/linkedin-acceptance-rate) |
| Reply rate | People who reply divided by people who accepted | Whether the first message works |
| Conversations that lead somewhere | Calls, demos, interviews booked | The only number that matters in the end |

Change one thing at a time, such as the note, the audience or the follow-up, so you know what moved.

## 8. Mistakes that sink outreach

- **Pitching in the first message.** It reads as spam and gets ignored or reported.
- **Identical text to everyone.** People notice. Use their name, their company or something real.
- **Too many people at once.** Volume without relevance lowers acceptance, which raises risk.
- **No follow-up.** One message is rarely enough.
- **Ignoring replies.** A reply is the goal. Answer quickly, from one inbox.

## 9. A 30-day plan

1. **Week 1.** Define your list, fix your profile, write two connection notes and a first message. Send to 30 to 50 people by hand.
2. **Week 2.** Read the results. Change the weakest part. Add a follow-up.
3. **Week 3.** Widen the list a little. Move repeated steps into a campaign if you want help with the typing.
4. **Week 4.** Review replies, calls and acceptance. Keep what worked, drop the rest.
